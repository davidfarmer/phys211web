Example 8.4.3 A complete cycle.

Consider a fixed amount of an ideal gas of diatomic molecules undergoing the processes shown in Figure 59. The gas starts at point A in the diagram and expands along the path \(\textbf{A}\longrightarrow{B}\) with no heat flowing into or out of the gas.
🔗
(a) Calculate the pressure of the gas at point B after the expansion. (b) Determine the net work done on the gas and the heat flow \(Q_\text{in}\) in one complete cycle (\(\textbf{A}\longrightarrow{B}\longrightarrow{C}\longrightarrow{A}\)).
🔗
Solution: (a) Since the process \(\textbf{A} \rightarrow \textbf{B}\) is adiabatic, \(pV^\gamma = (\text{constant} )\) and \(p_AV_A^\gamma = p_BV_B^\gamma\) so
\begin{equation} p_B = p_A\left(\frac{V_A}{V_B}\right)^{7/5} = 100 \,\text{kPa} \times \left(\frac{1.0 \,\text{L} }{2.5 \,\text{L} }\right)^{7/5} = 27.7 \,\text{kPa} .\tag{8.4.3} \end{equation}
🔗
(b) We will calculate the work done on the gas for each of the three processes and add up the results to get the net work. To figure out the total heat flow \(Q_\text{in}\) we could do something similar — i.e., calculate \(Q_\text{in}\) for each process and then add them up. But there is an easier way: since we know that \(\Delta E_\text{therm} = 0\) for the complete cycle, once we have determined the total \(W_\text{on}\) for the complete cycle, we can use the First Law of Thermodynamics to get \(Q_\text{in}\) for the complete cycle.
🔗
Let’s start with process \(\textbf{A} \rightarrow {B}\text{.}\) This is an adiabatic process, so \(Q_\text{in} = 0\text{.}\) Calculating work is tricky for adiabatic processes, so we’ll find \(\Delta E_\text{therm}\) first and then use the First Law to get \(W_\text{on}\text{.}\) This is a diatomic gas, so \(f = 5\) and \(\Delta E_\text{therm} = \frac{f}{2}nR\Delta T\text{.}\) \(\Delta E_\text{therm}\) can be found with the pressures and volumes given (see (8.2.3)):
\begin{align*} \Delta E_\text{therm} =\mathstrut \amp \frac{f}{2}(p_BV_B - p_AV_A)\\ =\mathstrut \amp \frac{5}{2} \left(27.7 \,\text{kPa} \times 2.5 \,\text{L} - 100 \,\text{kPa} \times 1.0 \,\text{L} \right)\\ =\mathstrut \amp -76.7 \,\text{J} . \end{align*}
🔗
We can now find the work done on the gas via the First Law:
\begin{align*} W_\text{on} =\mathstrut \amp \Delta E_\text{therm} - Q_\text{in}\\ =\mathstrut \amp -76.7 \,\text{J} - 0\\ =\mathstrut \amp -76.7 \,\text{J} . \end{align*}
🔗
Process \(\textbf{B} \rightarrow \textbf{C}\) is a constant-volume process (isochoric), so \(W_\text{on} = 0\text{.}\)
🔗
Process \(\textbf{C} \rightarrow {A}\) is a constant-pressure process (isobaric), so
\begin{align*} W_\text{on} =\mathstrut \amp -p\,\Delta V\\ =\mathstrut \amp -p\, (V_A - V_C)\\ =\mathstrut \amp -100 \,\text{kPa} \, (1.0 \,\text{L} - 2.5 \,\text{L} )\\ =\mathstrut \amp 150 \,\text{J} . \end{align*}
🔗
So, the net work \(W_\text{on}\) done on the gas for the complete cycle \(\textbf{A} \rightarrow \textbf{B} \rightarrow \textbf{C} \rightarrow \textbf{A}\) is
\begin{align*} W_\text{on} ^\text{net} =\mathstrut \amp W_\text{on} ^{A\to B}+W_\text{on} ^{B\to C}+W_\text{on} ^{C\to A}\\ =\mathstrut \amp -76.7 \,\text{J} + 0 + 150 \,\text{J}\\ =\mathstrut \amp 73.3 \,\text{J} . \end{align*}
🔗
As for the total heat flow \(Q_\text{in}\) for the entire cycle, we can use the First Law:
\begin{align*} Q_\text{in} =\mathstrut \amp \Delta E_\text{therm} - W_\text{on}\\ =\mathstrut \amp 0 - 73.3 \,\text{J}\\ =\mathstrut \amp -73.3 \,\text{J} . \end{align*}
🔗
This means the net heat flow during the entire cycle is \(73.3\,\text{J}\) flowing out of the gas.
