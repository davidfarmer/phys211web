Example 11.4.5. Automobile Efficiency.

The internal combustion engine of an automobile is a heat engine. Yes, there is gas being consumed, but that’s being burned to provide the high temperature of the hot reservoir. From there on, the engine functions as a heat engine.
🔗
The hot reservoir is about \(820^\circ{C}\) and the cold reservoir is almost air temperature, but typically more like \(70^\circ{C}\text{.}\) Burning a gallon of gasoline provides about \(120{MJ}\) of heat. What is the upper limit on how much work can be extracted from a gallon of gas, and how much heat must be dumped?
🔗
Solution: The moment you see the words “upper limit” (or similar language), then you can pull out the second law of thermodynamics: \(\Delta S_\text{ total } = 0\text{.}\) For this problem, \(\Delta S_\text{ total } = \Delta S_H + \Delta S_C\text{,}\) since the reservoirs are the only parts of this system whose entropy changes.
🔗
To calculate \(\Delta S\) for the reservoirs, we need to convert the temperatures to Kelvin, so \(T_H=820+273=1093{K}\) and \(T_C=70+273=343{K}\text{.}\) The entropy change of the hot reservoir is
\begin{equation} \Delta S_H = \frac{-|Q_H|}{T_H} = -\frac{120\times 10^6{J}}{1093{K}} = -1.10\times 10^{5}{J/K}\tag{11.4.12} \end{equation}
🔗
Therefore, since \(\Delta S_\text{ total } = 0\text{,}\) it follows that \(\Delta S_C\) must be positive by at least this amount.
\begin{equation} \Delta S_{C,{ min}} = \frac{|Q_{C,{ min}}|}{T_C} = 1.10\times 10^5{J/K}\tag{11.4.13} \end{equation}
so
\begin{equation} |Q_{C,{ min}}| = 343{K} \cdot 1.10\times 10^{5}{J/K} = 37.7{MJ}.\tag{11.4.14} \end{equation}
🔗
We get the maximum work by subtracting this from \(|Q_H|\text{:}\)
\begin{equation} |W_\text{ max } | = 120 - 37.7 = 82{MJ}.\tag{11.4.15} \end{equation}
🔗
In reality, the work output is only about 1/3 of this amount, due to (i) design choices to get the work out faster, i.e. to get more power, (ii) friction, and (iii) imperfect combustion of the fuel.
