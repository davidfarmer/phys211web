    Problem 0.3 Falling Birdie.

    Consider a badminton birdie that is falling under the influence of gravity and air resistance. Assume that the vertical acceleration of this birdie is given by
    \begin{equation*} a_y = \frac{dv}{dt} = g - bv, \end{equation*}
    where \(g = 9.8\,\text{m} /\text{s} ^2\) and \(b\) is some constant that depends on the mass and shape of the birdie along with properties of the air, and \(v\) is the instantaneous velocity of the bird (positive if the velocity is down and negative if the velocity is up). Suppose that the birdie is released from rest at time \(t=0\text{.}\)
    1. Discuss qualitatively how the speed of the birdie varies with time, given your knowledge of the relationship between the acceleration and the rate of change of the velocity. What is the velocity when the acceleration is zero? (This is called the terminal velocity.) What happens to the velocity after the acceleration reaches zero?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Without solving the equation above, sketch \(v(t)\) vs. \(t\text{.}\) This can be done as follows: at \(t=0\text{,}\) \(v\) is zero and the slope is \(g\text{.}\) Sketch a straight-line segment, neglecting any change in the slope for a short time interval. At the end of the interval, the velocity is no longer zero, so the slope is no longer \(g\text{.}\) Sketch another straight-line segment with the qualitatively appropriate slope for the next short time interval. Continue until your sketch shows the birdie has reached terminal velocity. What is the slope of the line after the birdie has reached terminal velocity?

    Problem 0.4 Estimating Velocities: Muzzle Speed of a Blow Dart.

    Be careful! Students in the past have set off the sprinkler and fire alarms in their dorms! Consider doing this outside or somewhere where the ceiling is very high.
    1. To estimate the “muzzle speed” of a blow dart, fire the dart straight up into the air and time how long it takes to come back down. Roughly half of that time is the time for the object to decelerate from its initial velocity to motionless (at the top of its motion). Warning: students often over-estimate the time required for the trip. Remember to count “0” when you fire the dart (“0 and 1 and 2 and …”)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Now, to estimate the initial velocity use that the acceleration is approximately constant, therefore
      \begin{equation*} a_y= \frac{{ d}v_y}{{ d}t} \approx \frac{\Delta v_y}{\Delta t} \end{equation*}
      . Keep this estimate handy: you’ll use it later on in this unit.

    Problem 0.12 Tension: Playing with an Elastic String with Bead.

    1. Attach one end of the elastic white string to the wooden bead. Take the string with bead and hang it straight down with the bead at the bottom. Wait for it to stop moving. Note the length of the string (you don’t have to measure it). Question: How does the tension in the string at this moment compare to the weight \(mg\) of the bead? Is \(T \lt mg\text{,}\) does \(T =mg\text{,}\) or is \(T> mg\text{?}\) Now, add the weight of the round metal spring to the bead. (One way to do this: put the bead inside in the middle of the spring and thread the string out between the coils.) Let the system hang motionless (hold the string with the bead and spring at the bottom pulling downward). Comment on the length of the elastic string now. What is the tension in the string now (answer qualitatively — no numbers)?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Qualitatively, what happens to the length of the string when the tension increases? What happens to the length of the string when the tension decreases? What do you think will happen to the string if the tension goes to zero?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Now let the weight and bead hang straight down. Once the system is motionless, pull the top of the string upward, accelerating the system momentarily. What happens to the string’s length? What does this mean about the tension in the string when the bead is accelerated upward? Do the same thing, but this time accelerate the bead/weight downward (quickly lower your hand holding the top of the string). What happens to the tension in the elastic string when the bead accelerates downward?
      🔗
      🔗

Do you observe any changes in the string in either case? What do you think is happening to the tension in the string in both cases?     5. A very common misconception is that tension for a hanging object is always equivalent to its weight \(mg\text{.}\) This is not true if the object is accelerating!!
